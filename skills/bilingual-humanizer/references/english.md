# English editing decisions

These are editorial heuristics, not forensic tests. Apply them to the actual audience and source voice. Standard grammar, second-language simplicity, predictable wording, and formal prose are compatible with human authorship.

## Rebuild the argument

Identify each paragraph's job before editing its sentences. Bring a delayed request or finding forward; retain background where the reader needs it. Combine paragraphs that repeat the same point, and split a paragraph that conceals different conditions or actors. Do not turn every paragraph into a short declaration plus a dramatic fragment.

| Candidate | Useful edit | Reason to keep it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock opening about a rapidly changing world | Begin with the source's actual topic or request | The historical change is itself part of the argument |
| Repeated previews and recap sentences | State a point once, where it belongs | Navigation in a long report or a spoken presentation |
| Empty significance claims | Remove empty framing; retain a stated evaluation and its reason in direct language | A sourced evaluation or the writer's real opinion; do not erase `this matters because X` merely because it sounds formulaic |
| Promotional superlatives | Preserve the actual claim and remove redundant inflation | Approved brand language, attributed speech, or evidence-backed comparison |
| Abstract nominalization chains | Recover an existing actor and a precise verb | A defined term or the object is the proper focus |
| Copula avoidance | Prefer a natural `is`, `has`, or direct verb | `constitutes` or `represents` has a distinct technical meaning |
| Repeated negative contrasts | Keep the positive claim if the negative adds nothing | Both sides distinguish real alternatives or correct a misconception |
| Forced three-part phrasing | Keep only source-supported distinctions | A genuine three-item list, including all three items |
| Generic transitions | Let an obvious relation stand without a label | The connector specifies causation, concession, order, or inference |
| Overloaded hedging | Express the original uncertainty once | Necessary scientific or contractual qualification |
| Vague attribution | Preserve attribution and flag missing evidence if material | Do not manufacture a named authority |
| Repeated synonyms for one technical term | Use the established term consistently | Ordinary narrative variation that does not change the referent |
| Excessive bold labels | Restore readable paragraphs or parallel lists | The deliverable relies on scan-friendly labels |
| Chatbot residue | Remove conversation scaffolding from the deliverable | Quoted dialogue, examples, or an intentionally conversational document |
| Generic closing offer | End after the needed action or information | An actual offer, commitment, invitation, or correspondence convention |

Words such as `robust`, `leverage`, `comprehensive`, `significant`, `delve`, and `moreover` invite contextual review, not automatic substitution. Statistical significance is not decorative importance. Financial leverage is not a synonym for using something. Do not simplify a specialist's exact term into an inaccurate familiar one.

## Voice and syntax

- Preserve the writer's variety of English and consistent spelling. Do not assume American English.
- Use contractions, fragments, idiom, and humor only where the source voice and audience support them. Do not equate casual English with natural English.
- Passive voice is appropriate when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately outside focus. Never supply `we` just to make the sentence active.
- Let punctuation clarify relationships. Keep purposeful dashes, semicolons, colons, parentheses, and lists. There is no punctuation blacklist.
- Use long sentences for tightly related qualifications and short sentences for a direct point. Do not target a sentence-length distribution or insert random unusual vocabulary.
- Preserve a real hesitation or unresolved opinion. Do not add performed candor such as a confession the writer never made.
- Retain legally or technically meaningful `shall`, `must`, `may`, `should`, `only`, `unless`, `at least`, `up to`, and `respectively`. Rephrase them only when their force remains identical and the task permits it.

## Active rewrite example

This synthetic example is not quoted from an upstream skill.

Source:

> It is important to note that our team conducted an evaluation of the cache in 48 trials. The median response time was 120 ms before the change and 90 ms after the change. These findings demonstrate the remarkable potential of the approach. However, the trials did not include mobile clients, and the result should not be generalized to them.

Rewrite:

> Our team's 48 cache trials show the approach's strong potential: median response time fell from 120 ms to 90 ms after the change. Mobile clients were excluded, so the result should not be generalized to them.

This integrates the author's positive evaluation into the result instead of keeping a second sentence that comments on it. It does not change `median` to `average`, claim statistical significance, invent user benefits, or extrapolate to mobile clients. Evaluations need not disappear just because they are enthusiastic; preserve an actual stance without strengthening its evidentiary basis. The colon is optional, not a style template.

## Keep when already effective

`Please send the draft by Friday. I need time to review the figures.` needs no cosmetic change. Neither a detectable pattern nor the fact that a skill was invoked creates a reason to revise a sound sentence.
