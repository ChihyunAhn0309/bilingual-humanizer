Why our repair table now starts with a question

At Saturday's courtyard repair table, we used to start by opening up whatever someone brought. It felt efficient. Then we spent most of a morning dismantling a desk lamp whose owner mainly wanted a longer cable.

Organizer Lena Ortiz changed that this month by introducing a short intake conversation. Volunteers now ask what the object used to do, what changed, and what a useful result would look like.

During our first 90 minutes, we accepted 12 items and set aside three that needed specialist tools. According to Lena's handwritten tally, more jobs were left unfinished at the previous session. Attendance was higher then too, so the comparison is imperfect.

If you are bringing an item, bring its loose parts with it and tell us about the last time it worked. You do not need to diagnose the fault. A photograph showing how it fits into your home can also explain why an apparently simple replacement would be inconvenient.

My favorite part of the change is that we now treat the owner's frustration as useful information. Repair is often described as rescuing objects from waste. Sometimes it starts with rescuing a person from advice that solves the wrong problem.
