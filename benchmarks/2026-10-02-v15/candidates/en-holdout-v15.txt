Why our repair table now starts with a question

At Saturday's courtyard repair table, we used to start by opening whatever someone brought. That felt efficient until we spent most of a morning taking apart a desk lamp whose owner mainly wanted a longer cable.

This month, organizer Lena Ortiz introduced a short intake conversation. Before starting work, volunteers ask what the object used to do, what changed, and what a useful result would look like. In our first 90 minutes, we accepted 12 items and set aside three that needed specialist tools. According to Lena's handwritten tally, we left more jobs unfinished at the previous session. Attendance was higher then, though, so the comparison is imperfect.

If you are bringing something along, include its loose parts and tell us about the last time it worked. You do not need to diagnose the fault. A photograph showing how the item fits into your home can also explain why even an apparently simple replacement would be inconvenient.

My favorite change is that the owner's frustration now counts as useful information. Repair is often described as rescuing objects from waste. Sometimes it starts with rescuing a person from advice that solves the wrong problem.
